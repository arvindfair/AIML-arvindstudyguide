Simple Exponential Smoothing


In the previous session, you learnt how to build simple forecasting models such as the naïve model, the simple average model, and the simple moving average model. In all these models, past values influence future forecasts, but the way this influence is handled is quite limited.

 

In the naïve model, only the most recent value is used. In the simple average model, all historical values are given equal importance. In the simple moving average model, a fixed number of recent values are considered, but each value in the window still receives equal weight. This raises an important question: should all past values influence future forecasts equally?

 

Let's consider monthly sales data for a company over several years. While older sales values may have some influence, more recent sales are usually more relevant for predicting future values. This pattern is common in many real-world time series datasets. To capture this behaviour, we need models that assign more weight to recent values and less weight to older ones. Exponential smoothing provides a way to do this.

# 1. First understand the problem

Suppose you have monthly sales:

| Month | Sales |
| ----- | ----: |
| Jan   |   100 |
| Feb   |   110 |
| Mar   |   105 |
| Apr   |   120 |
| May   |   125 |

You want to predict **June sales**.

The basic question is:

> **How much importance should we give to each previous month?**

Different forecasting methods answer this differently.

---

# 2. Three methods you already know

### Method 1: Naive

Use **only the latest value**.

May = 125

So:

**June forecast = 125**

Here, May gets 100% importance.

---

### Method 2: Simple Average

Take the average of everything:

$$
\frac{100+110+105+120+125}{5}=112
$$

So:

**June forecast = 112**

Problem?

January's 100 gets exactly the same importance as May's 125.

But intuitively, May may be more useful because it is recent.

---

### Method 3: Moving Average

Suppose we use the last 3 months:

$$
\frac{105+120+125}{3}=116.67
$$

Again, all three months get **equal weight**.

---

# 3. Exponential Smoothing — the main idea

This is the important concept.

Instead of saying:

> "Use only the latest value."

or

> "Give every value equal importance."

we say:

> **Recent values should matter more, and older values should matter less.**

For example:

| Observation |  Weight |
| ----------- | ------: |
| May         |    0.50 |
| April       |    0.25 |
| March       |   0.125 |
| February    |  0.0625 |
| January     | 0.03125 |

Notice something?

The weights keep getting smaller as we go backwards.

That's why it is called **exponential smoothing**.

The course specifically describes SES as assigning the largest weight to recent observations, with weights decreasing exponentially for older observations. 

---

# 4. What is Alpha (α)?

This is probably the **most important thing to understand**.

Alpha controls:

> **How much should I listen to the latest data?**

α is between 0 and 1.

### α = 0.8

Very high importance to recent data.

The model says:

> "I really care about what just happened."

So the forecast reacts quickly to changes.

### α = 0.2

Low importance to recent data.

The model says:

> "Don't react too quickly. Look at historical information."

So the forecast changes slowly.

The course states that a larger α makes the forecast more responsive to recent changes, while a smaller α produces smoother forecasts that rely more on historical values. 

---

# 5. The easiest formula to remember

Simple Exponential Smoothing can be understood as:

$$
\boxed{New\ Level = \alpha(Actual) +(1-\alpha)(Old\ Level)}
$$

Don't get scared by the formula.

It simply means:

> **New estimate = some portion of today's actual value + remaining portion of yesterday's estimate.**

That's all.

---

# 9. Why does it become "exponential"?

Suppose:

$$
\alpha=0.8
$$

The latest observation gets approximately:

$$
0.8
$$

Previous one:

$$
0.8(1-0.8)=0.16

Previous:

Previous:

$$

So:

**0.8 → 0.16 → 0.032 → 0.0064**

The weights fall very quickly.

That's the **exponential decrease** mentioned in your material.

# 10. Now the BIG concept: Level, Trend and Seasonality

This is where your course starts becoming confusing.

Think of a time series as potentially having **three things**:

### Level

> Where is the data generally sitting?

### Trend

> Is it going upward or downward?

### Seasonality

> Does a pattern repeat periodically?

For example, imagine a company's sales:

```text
Sales
 ^
 |                 /\       /\
 |        /\      /  \     /  \
 |   /\  /  \    /    \   /    \
 |__/  \/    \__/      \_/      \__
 +----------------------------------> Time
```

There may be:

* a general average → **Level**
* an upward movement → **Trend**
* repeating peaks → **Seasonality**

Your course describes exponential smoothing models as capturing these components. 

---

This is the part I want you to remember for your exam.

**Level**

Suppose your sales are:


Clearly there is an **upward trend**.

But SES only understands:

> "What is the current level?"

It doesn't explicitly understand:

> "The sales are increasing by around 5 every period."

Therefore, SES can lag behind the actual data.

That's why the course shows SES performing poorly when the series has additional structure such as seasonality/change in level.

# 14. One more important concept: Forecast vs Prediction

Your professor spends time on this, but the simple way to remember it is:

### Prediction

Predicting an unknown value.

Example:

> Given customer age, income, etc., predict whether they will buy.

### Forecast

Predicting a **future time-series value**.

Example:

> Given sales from January–December, forecast January next year.

So:

**Forecasting is a type of prediction, but prediction is not necessarily forecasting.**

Your course makes exactly this distinction. 

---

# 15. How do we choose α?

This is another common exam question.

You don't normally just randomly decide:

> α = 0.2

Instead, different α values can be evaluated using forecasting error measures, and the value producing better forecasting performance can be selected.

For example:

```text
α = 0.1 → RMSE = 12.5
α = 0.2 → RMSE = 10.2
α = 0.3 → RMSE = 9.1
α = 0.4 → RMSE = 11.4
```

Then α = 0.3 gives the lowest RMSE among these tested values.

The course also discusses evaluating forecasts using measures such as RMSE and MAPE.

In this part of the demonstration, we will fit exponential smoothing models to our data. The models that we will build are:
- Simple exponential smoothing: Captures level in the data
- Holt's exponential smoothing: Captures level and trend in the data
- Holt Winters' exponential smoothing: Captures level, trend, and seasonality in the data

**Note:** These models work on the assumption that more recent values in a time series have more influence on future values in the series than older values in the data.

### Subpart 1 -  Simple Exponential Smoothing

Now, we will get into our first exponential smoothing method, the simple exponential smoothing model.

**Note:** This model aims to capture the level in the time series data.

To apply this method we will first import the *SimpleExpSmoothing* function from *statsmodels*. This function creates an exponentially-weighted moving average model for a time series data set, where more recent observations are given more weight than older ones. It takes as input the endogenous variable of the training data set and returns a model object that can be used to make predictions on new data.

# Import 'SimpleExpSmoothing' from 'statsmodels' for simple exponential smoothing
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import SimpleExpSmoothing

# Create a model instance for the training data
model = SimpleExpSmoothing(endog = df_train['Sales'])


Now we will use the *fit* function to fit the model object on the training data set. A couple of parameters that should be emphasised here are:
- *smoothing_level*: This is the same as $\alpha$. It specifies the coefficient for level smoothing.
- *optimized*: This parameter determines whether or not to use an optimisation algorithm to estimate parameters such as *smoothing_level* (if set to *True*) or to consider user-specified values (if set to *False*).

**Note:** The default value of *optimized* is *True*.

# Fit the model object on the training data and obtain predictions
model = model.fit(smoothing_level = 0.3, optimized = False)
y_pred_ses = model.forecast(len(df_test))

The above result is for an $\alpha$ value of 0.3. We can, in fact, set the *optimized* parameter to *False* and tune for $\alpha$. Let's define a couple of helper functions to do this.


# Define a helper function that builds and evaluates a simple exponential smoothing model for the data
# Note: The 'smoothing_level' parameter will be user-controlled
def eval_sesm(train_data = None, test_data = None, alpha_val = 0):

    sesm_model = SimpleExpSmoothing(endog = train_data['Sales'])
    sesm_model = sesm_model.fit(smoothing_level = alpha_val, optimized = False)
    y_pred_sesm = sesm_model.forecast(len(test_data))

    y_pred_sesm_list = [y_pred_sesm[0]] * len(test_data)
    sesm_rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_true = test_data['Sales'], y_pred = y_pred_sesm_list))
    sesm_mape = np.mean(np.abs(test_data['Sales'] - y_pred_sesm_list) / test_data['Sales']) * 100

    sesm_rmse = np.round(sesm_rmse, 2)
    sesm_mape = np.round(sesm_mape, 2)

    return(sesm_rmse, sesm_mape)


# Define a helper function that evaluates the performance of a range of models and displays the results visually
# Note: The range of alpha values will be user-controlled
def show_sesm_performance(train_data = None, test_data = None, alpha_list = [0]):
    rmse_list = []
    mape_list = []

    for alpha in alpha_list:
        rmse_list.append(eval_sesm(train_data = train_data, test_data = test_data, alpha_val = alpha)[0])
        mape_list.append(eval_sesm(train_data = train_data, test_data = test_data, alpha_val = alpha)[1])

    performance_df = pd.DataFrame(index = alpha_list, data = {'RMSE': rmse_list, 'MAPE': mape_list})

    plt.figure(figsize = (14, 4))
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
    sns.lineplot(data = performance_df, x = performance_df.index, y = rmse_list)
    plt.xlabel('alpha')
    plt.ylabel('RMSE')
    plt.title('RMSE as a function of smoothing level')
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
    sns.lineplot(data = performance_df, x = performance_df.index, y = mape_list)
    plt.xlabel('alpha')
    plt.ylabel('MAPE')
    plt.title('MAPE as a function of smoothing level')

    return(None)

Yes. Let’s forget the complicated equations for a moment and understand **Holt’s Exponential Smoothing (HESM)** from your course as if we are solving a real business problem.

The key difference from SES is very simple: **SES understands the current level, while Holt understands both the current level and the direction in which the data is moving (trend).** Your course explicitly says Holt captures **level + trend**, but not seasonality. 

## 1. First, why do we need Holt?

Imagine website traffic:

| Month | Visitors |
| ----- | -------: |
| Jan   |      100 |
| Feb   |      110 |
| Mar   |      120 |
| Apr   |      130 |
| May   |      140 |

Look at the data:

```text
140 |                 ●
130 |             ●
120 |         ●
110 |     ●
100 | ●
    +--------------------
      Jan Feb Mar Apr May
```

What do you see?

**The traffic is increasing.**

There are two pieces of information here:

### Level

Where are we currently?

Approximately **140**.

### Trend

Which direction are we moving?

Approximately **+10 per month**.

---

# 2. What does SES do?

Simple Exponential Smoothing basically says:

> "I'll keep track of the current level."

So it might understand:

**Current level ≈ 140**

But it doesn't explicitly keep track of:

**Trend = +10 per month**

That's the limitation of SES.

Your course describes this as the core limitation of SES: it cannot explicitly model trend. 

---

# 3. Holt's big idea

Holt says:

> "I need to remember TWO things."

### Thing 1 → Level

Where am I now?

### Thing 2 → Trend

Where am I going?

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Holt = Level + Trend}}
$$

That's the entire concept.

---

# 4. Think of driving a car 🚗

This is the easiest analogy.

Suppose you're driving.

You need to know:

**Where am I?**

That's **LEVEL**.

But you also need to know:

**Am I moving forward or backward, and how quickly?**

That's **TREND**.

If you only know your location but don't know your speed/direction, predicting where you'll be next is difficult.

Holt keeps both pieces of information.

---

# 5. The two parameters

Because Holt has two components, it has two smoothing parameters.

### α (alpha)

Controls **level**.

> How much should I pay attention to the latest actual value when updating the level?

### β (beta)

Controls **trend**.

> How much should I pay attention to the latest change in the trend?

Your course states that both α and β range from 0 to 1. 

So:

```text
HOLT
 |
 +---- Level  → α
 |
 +---- Trend  → β
```

---

# 6. Now let's understand the forecast

This is the most important Holt concept.

Suppose the model has calculated:

**Current level = 140**

and

**Current trend = +10**

What should next month's forecast be?

Obviously:

$$
140+10=150
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Forecast}=\text{Level}+\text{Trend}}
$$

This is exactly what your course says: the next-timestamp prediction is the sum of the predicted current level and predicted current trend. 

---

This is why Holt is useful for trending data.

# 9. Now the equations will make sense

Your course gives complicated-looking equations.

Don't memorize them blindly.

There are really only two jobs.

### Job 1: Update the LEVEL

The new level considers:

* current actual value
* previous level
* previous trend
* α

Conceptually:

> **New level = current information + previous level/trend information**

---

### Job 2: Update the TREND

The new trend considers:

* how the level changed
* previous trend
* β

Conceptually:

> **New trend = recent change + previous trend information**

The course explicitly explains that Holt's equations recursively build **L₂, T₂, then L₃, T₃, etc.** 

So don't look at the equations as something completely new.

They're just doing:

```text
Actual data
     ↓
Update Level
     ↓
Update Trend
     ↓
Forecast
     ↓
New actual arrives
     ↓
Update Level
     ↓
Update Trend
     ↓
New Forecast
```

Holt does **NOT** model this.

# 12. Additive vs Multiplicative — don't mix this up

Your course also introduces:

```text
seasonal = additive / multiplicative / None
```

For Holt, **seasonal = None** because Holt isn't modeling seasonality. 

For the trend, you can have:

The data increases by roughly the **same amount**.

Example:

```text

130
```

Increase:

**+10, +10, +10, +10**

That's additive-like trend.

The data increases by roughly the **same percentage**.

Example:

Approximately:

**+10%, +10%, +10%, +10%**

Your course's example uses an additive trend for a nearly linear constant increase.

### Subpart 2 - Holt's Method

Since exponential smoothing equations are recursive, there is no closed-form solution for these parameters. Instead, an iterative numerical optimisation procedure is used, where parameter values are repeatedly updated until the forecast error cannot be reduced further.

 

We also learnt about a more formal approach, which is provided by the ETS (Error–Trend–Seasonal) framework, which treats exponential smoothing models as statistical state-space models. The level, trend, and seasonal components are viewed as unobserved states, and parameters are estimated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

 

The ETS framework also enables the construction of statistically sound prediction intervals and provides a systematic way to classify models using the ETS taxonomy, such as:

 

ETS(A,N,N) for Simple Exponential Smoothing

ETS(A,A,N) for Holt’s method

ETS(A,A,M) for Holt–Winters with multiplicative seasonality

 

where N stands for none, A stands for additive, and M stands for multiplicative, referring to the error, trend, and seasonal components, respectively.
