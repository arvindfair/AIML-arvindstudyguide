# 1. First: What is autocorrelation?

Suppose your daily website traffic is:

```text
Monday     100
Tuesday    110
Wednesday  105
Thursday   120
Friday     125
Saturday   130
```

In normal correlation, we might ask:

> Are **X and Y** related?

In **autocorrelation**, there is only **one variable**, but we compare it with **its own past values**.

For example:

```text
Today's traffic  ↔ Yesterday's traffic
```

or

```text
Today's traffic  ↔ Traffic 2 days ago
```

That's why it is called:

> **AUTO-correlation = correlation with yourself at different time points.**

---

# 2. What is a lag?

This is the MOST important concept.

Suppose:

```text
Time:       1    2    3    4    5
Sales:     10   20   30   40   50
```

### Lag 1

Compare each value with the value **one time step before it**:

```text
Current       Previous
  20    ↔       10
  30    ↔       20
  40    ↔       30
  50    ↔       40
```

So:

> **Lag 1 = compare today's value with yesterday's value.**

---

### Lag 2

Compare each value with the value **two time steps before**:

```text
Current       2 steps before

  30    ↔       10
  40    ↔       20
  50    ↔       30
```

So:

> **Lag 2 = compare today's value with the value from 2 periods ago.**

---

### Lag 3

```text
  40 ↔ 10
  50 ↔ 20
```

Therefore:

```text
Lag 1 → previous observation
Lag 2 → 2 observations back
Lag 3 → 3 observations back
...
```

The lecture uses **K** to represent the lag.

So:

$$
K=1
$$

means one period back.

---

# 3. What does autocorrelation actually tell us?

Suppose:

```text
Yesterday's sales = 100
Today's sales = 105
```

And suppose this happens repeatedly:

```text
Yesterday high → Today high
Yesterday low  → Today low
```

Then there is **positive autocorrelation**.

It means:

> Past values and current values tend to move in the same direction.

---

### Positive autocorrelation

```text
Past high → Current high
Past low  → Current low
```

Correlation is positive.

For example:

$$
\rho_1 = 0.8
$$

means strong positive autocorrelation at lag 1.

---

### Negative autocorrelation

Suppose:

```text
Yesterday high → Today low
Yesterday low  → Today high
```

Then:

$$
\rho_1 < 0
$$

For example:

$$
\rho_1=-0.7
$$

means strong negative autocorrelation.

---

### No autocorrelation

If knowing yesterday's value doesn't tell us anything useful about today's value:

$$
\rho_1 \approx 0
$$

there is little/no linear autocorrelation at that lag.

---

# 4. Why do we care about autocorrelation?

Because **time-series observations are often not independent**.

For example:

```text
Today's sales
      ↑
Yesterday's sales
      ↑
Day before yesterday
```

Past observations may contain information about future observations.

That's exactly what time-series models try to exploit.

So autocorrelation helps us answer:

> **"How much does the past tell us about the present?"**

---

# 5. What is ACF?

ACF stands for:

> **Autocorrelation Function**

It calculates autocorrelation at **different lags**.

For example:

| Lag | Autocorrelation |
| --: | --------------: |
|   0 |            1.00 |
|   1 |            0.72 |
|   2 |            0.51 |
|   3 |            0.30 |
|   4 |            0.10 |
|   5 |           -0.02 |

The ACF plot simply puts this information on a graph:

```text
Autocorrelation
     |
 1.0 | ●
     |
 0.7 |     ●
     |
 0.5 |         ●
     |
 0.3 |             ●
     |
 0.0 |-------------------------
     |  0   1   2   3   4   5
                 Lag
```

So remember:

> **ACF = correlation at different lags.**

---

# 6. Why is lag 0 always 1?

This is extremely important for exams.

At lag 0:

```text
Today's value ↔ Today's value
```

You are correlating the series with **itself**.

Anything compared with itself has correlation:

$$
1
$$

Therefore:

$$
ACF(0)=1
$$

Always.

---

# 7. Understanding the ACF formula

Your lecture gives the basic idea:

$$
\rho_k =
\frac{\text{Autocovariance at lag }k}
{\text{Variance}}
$$

Don't worry about memorizing the complicated formula immediately.

Understand the structure:

```text
                 Relationship
ACF =   ----------------------------
                 Variability
```

The numerator asks:

> Do the current values and lagged values move together?

The denominator normalizes it.

That's why ACF is between:

$$
-1 \leq \rho_k \leq 1
$$

---

# 8. Let's understand the formula intuitively

Suppose the mean is:

$$
\bar Y=30
$$

At some time:

```text
Current value = 40
Previous value = 35
```

We look at their deviations from the mean:

```text
Current:
40 - 30 = +10

Previous:
35 - 30 = +5
```

Both are positive.

So:

```text
(+10)(+5) = positive
```

This contributes to **positive autocorrelation**.

Now suppose:

```text
Current = 40
Previous = 20
```

Then:

```text
40 - 30 = +10
20 - 30 = -10
```

Therefore:

```text
(+10)(-10) = negative
```

This contributes to **negative autocorrelation**.

That's the intuition behind the numerator.

---

# 9. ACF example

Imagine:

```text
Lag 0 → 1.00
Lag 1 → 0.70
Lag 2 → 0.45
Lag 3 → 0.20
Lag 4 → 0.05
```

What does this tell us?

Lag 1:

$$
0.70
$$

means the current observation has a fairly strong positive relationship with the immediately previous observation.

Lag 2:

$$
0.45
$$

means there is still some relationship with two periods ago.

Lag 3:

$$
0.20
$$

weaker.

Lag 4:

$$
0.05
$$

almost no relationship.

So the ACF is showing us **how dependence decreases as we go further into the past**.

---

# 10. But there is one BIG problem with ACF

Suppose:

```text
Today's value
     ↑
Yesterday's value
     ↑
Day before yesterday
```

You might find:

```text
Today's value ↔ 2-days-ago value
```

is correlated.

But maybe that happens **only because yesterday's value connects them**.

For example:

```text
Y(t-2) → Y(t-1) → Y(t)
```

There may not be a direct relationship:

```text
Y(t-2) → Y(t)
```

ACF does not completely separate these direct and indirect relationships.

This is where **PACF** comes in.

---

# 11. PACF = Partial Autocorrelation Function

PACF asks:

> **"What is the direct relationship between the current value and a particular lag after removing the influence of the intermediate lags?"**

That's the key difference.

### ACF

Includes:

> **Direct + indirect relationships**

### PACF

Focuses on:

> **Direct relationship**

Your source explicitly describes PACF as controlling for the influence of intermediate lags.

---

# 12. Very simple example

Imagine:

```text
Monday → Tuesday → Wednesday
```

Suppose:

```text
Monday sales = 100
Tuesday sales = 110
Wednesday sales = 120
```

ACF may find:

```text
Wednesday ↔ Monday
```

correlated.

But maybe Wednesday is related to Monday only because:

```text
Monday → Tuesday → Wednesday
```

PACF asks:

> After accounting for Tuesday, is Monday still directly related to Wednesday?

That's the intuition.

---

# 13. ACF vs PACF

This is one table I would memorize.

| ACF                                    | PACF                                             |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Autocorrelation Function               | Partial Autocorrelation Function                 |
| Measures correlation at different lags | Measures direct correlation at a lag             |
| Includes direct + indirect effects     | Controls for intermediate lags                   |
| Helps identify dependence across lags  | Helps identify specific/direct lag relationships |
| Uses lagged versions of the series     | Removes influence of shorter/intermediate lags   |

---

# 14. What is the confidence interval on an ACF/PACF plot?

This part of your lecture is very important.

You will usually see something like:

```text
Correlation
    |
 1  |        |
    |        |
    |--------|--------  upper confidence boundary
    |        |
 0  |----------------
    |        |
    |--------|--------  lower confidence boundary
    |        |
 -1 |
       1  2  3  4  5
             Lag
```

The shaded region is typically a **95% confidence interval** in the example discussed in your source.

The basic interpretation is:

> If a spike falls outside the confidence band, the autocorrelation is considered statistically significant at that level.

If it falls inside:

> We generally don't consider it statistically significant at that level.

Your source gives exactly this interpretation for the ACF example: lag 1 looked relatively strong, but because it remained inside the confidence region, it was not considered statistically significant at the stated confidence level.

---

# 15. Don't make this mistake

Suppose:

```text
Lag 1 = 0.30
```

and:

```text
Lag 2 = 0.10
```

You might say:

> "0.30 is bigger, therefore lag 1 is important."

Not necessarily.

You must also look at the **confidence interval**.

For example:

```text
Lag 1 = 0.30
        ↓
inside confidence band
```

Then it may **not be statistically significant**.

But:

```text
Lag 6 = -0.45
          ↓
outside confidence band
```

Then lag 6 may be statistically significant.

This is why you should look at **both the height of the spike and the confidence band**.

---

# 16. Your lecture's PACF example

The source says the PACF plot showed:

> strong partial autocorrelation at **lag 6**.

So the interpretation is:

```text
PACF lag 6
     ↓
strong direct relationship
     ↓
after accounting for intermediate lags
```

That's different from simply saying:

> "Lag 6 has correlation."

It means lag 6 has a relatively strong **partial/direct relationship** according to the PACF plot.

# 17. Why ACF and PACF help us choose lag order

Suppose you're building a time-series model and you don't know:

```text
Should I use lag 1?
lag 2?
lag 3?
...
lag 10?
```

ACF/PACF plots give clues.

For example:

```text
Lag:       1    2    3    4    5    6
ACF:      .8   .6   .4   .2   .1   .0
```

This tells us there is dependence that gradually decreases.

PACF might show:

```text
Lag:       1    2    3    4    5    6
PACF:     .8   .1   .0   .0   .0   .0
```

This suggests the strongest direct relationship is at lag 1.

So ACF/PACF are useful for understanding **which lags may matter** when constructing time-series models.

---

# 18. One VERY important distinction

Don't think:

> "ACF tells me the exact lag order automatically."

Instead:

> **ACF and PACF provide evidence/clues about which lags have meaningful relationships.**

Then the model and validation process can be used to determine what actually works well.

### Question 2 — PACF

That's **PACF**.

# 20. Exam cheat sheet ⭐

### Autocorrelation

> Correlation between a time series and its own lagged values.

### Lag

> Number of time steps by which the series is shifted.

```text
Lag 1 → previous period
Lag 2 → two periods ago
Lag 3 → three periods ago
```

### ACF

> Measures autocorrelation at different lags, including both direct and indirect relationships.

### PACF

> Measures the direct relationship between the series and a lag after controlling for intermediate lags.

### Lag 0

$$
ACF(0)=1
$$

because the series is correlated with itself.

### Positive autocorrelation

```text
High → High
Low  → Low
```

### Negative autocorrelation

```text
High → Low
Low  → High
```

### Confidence interval

> Helps determine whether an observed autocorrelation is statistically significant.

### Most important difference

```text
             ACF                 PACF
              ↓                    ↓
       Direct + indirect       Direct effect
              ↓                    ↓
      "Are they related?"   "Are they directly related?"
```

### Memory trick

> **ACF = All Connections**
> **PACF = Pure/Partial direct Connection**

And the single sentence I'd memorize for your exam:

> **ACF measures the correlation of a time series with its lagged values, while PACF measures the direct correlation with a particular lag after removing the effects of intermediate lags.**

Absolutely. **PACF is one of those topics that sounds difficult because the phrase “controlling for intermediate lags” is confusing.** Let's understand it slowly with a concrete example.

## 1. First, forget the formula

The most important question PACF answers is:

> **“Is lag \(k\) directly related to the current value, after removing the effect of the lags in between?”**

That is the entire idea.

Your lecture describes PACF as focusing on the **direct correlation** between a time series and its lagged values while controlling for intermediate lags. 

---

# 2. Start with a simple time series

Suppose our daily sales are:

| Day | Sales |
| --- | ----: |
| 1   |   100 |
| 2   |   110 |
| 3   |   120 |
| 4   |   130 |
| 5   |   140 |
| 6   |   150 |

Suppose today is Day 6.

We can look backward:

```text
Today
  ↓
Day 6 = 150

Lag 1 → Day 5 = 140
Lag 2 → Day 4 = 130
Lag 3 → Day 3 = 120
Lag 4 → Day 2 = 110
Lag 5 → Day 1 = 100
```

---

# 3. What does ACF do?

ACF asks:

### Lag 1

```text
Day 6 ↔ Day 5
```

### Lag 2

```text
Day 6 ↔ Day 4
```

### Lag 3

```text
Day 6 ↔ Day 3
```

and so on.

So ACF essentially asks:

> **“How correlated is today's value with each previous value?”**

But there is a problem.

Suppose:

```text
Day 6 ← Day 5 ← Day 4
```

If Day 6 is correlated with Day 4, perhaps that relationship exists because Day 4 influences Day 5 and Day 5 influences Day 6.

So ACF can contain **indirect relationships**.

---

# 4. This is where PACF becomes useful

PACF says:

> "Okay, let's look at the relationship between Day 6 and Day 4, but let's first account for Day 5."

So:

```text
ACF:

Day 4 ───────────────→ Day 6
       direct + indirect


PACF:

Day 4 ───────────────→ Day 6
          ↓
      remove effect
      of Day 5
```

Then we ask:

> **Does Day 4 still have a relationship with Day 6?**

If yes → there is a direct relationship at lag 2.

If no → the relationship was mainly explained by the intermediate lag.

---

# 5. Let's make it even easier

Imagine you have:

```text
A → B → C
```

You observe:

```text
A and C are correlated.
```

Can you immediately say:

> A directly affects C?

No.

Maybe:

```text
A → B → C
```

The relationship between A and C may simply be transmitted through B.

PACF tries to answer:

> **After accounting for B, is A still directly related to C?**

That's exactly the intuition.

---

# 6. Now map this to time series

Suppose:

```text
Y(t)     = Today
Y(t-1)   = Yesterday
Y(t-2)   = 2 days ago
```

For **lag 2**, PACF asks approximately:

> What is the relationship between \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-2}\), after accounting for \(Y_{t-1}\)?

So:

```text
Y(t-2) → Y(t-1) → Y(t)

        remove this
             ↓
Y(t-2) ─────────→ Y(t)
         ?
```

The remaining relationship is what PACF is interested in.

---

# 7. ACF vs PACF using Lag 2

This is extremely important.

### ACF at lag 2

ACF asks:

> How correlated are \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-2}\)?

It doesn't remove the influence of \(Y_{t-1}\).

```text
Y(t-2) ─────────→ Y(t)
       ↑
       │
     through
       │
Y(t-1)
```

Therefore, the correlation can include:

```text
Direct effect
+
Indirect effect through lag 1
```

---

### PACF at lag 2

PACF asks:

> How correlated are \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-2}\) **after accounting for \(Y_{t-1}\)**?

So it tries to isolate:

```text
Direct relationship
```

---

# 8. What happens at different PACF lags?

This is where students often get confused.

### PACF at lag 1

There are **no intermediate lags**.

```text
Y(t-1) → Y(t)
```

So PACF lag 1 is essentially the same basic correlation relationship as lag-1 autocorrelation.

---

### PACF at lag 2

Control for lag 1:

```text
Y(t-2) → Y(t-1) → Y(t)

Remove/control Y(t-1)
```

Then measure the remaining relationship between:

```text
Y(t-2) ↔ Y(t)
```

---

### PACF at lag 3

Now control for:

```text
Lag 1
Lag 2
```

Conceptually:

```text
Y(t-3)
   ↓
Y(t-2)
   ↓
Y(t-1)
   ↓
Y(t)
```

PACF asks:

> After accounting for lags 1 and 2, is lag 3 still directly related to the current value?

---

### PACF at lag 4

Control for:

```text
Lag 1
Lag 2
Lag 3
```

Then measure the remaining relationship with lag 4.

---

# 9. So the pattern is

| PACF lag | Controls for intermediate lags |
| -------: | ------------------------------ |
|        1 | None                           |
|        2 | Lag 1                          |
|        3 | Lag 1, Lag 2                   |
|        4 | Lag 1, Lag 2, Lag 3            |
|        5 | Lag 1–4                        |

This is an excellent table to remember.

---

# 10. Now let's understand the actual calculation idea

You don't need the complicated mathematics initially.

Suppose we're calculating **PACF at lag 2**.

We have:

```text
Current value = Y(t)

Lag 1 = Y(t-1)

Lag 2 = Y(t-2)
```

First, we model the current value using lag 1:

$$
Y_t = c + \phi_1Y_{t-1} + error
$$

This captures the part of today's value that can be explained by yesterday.

Then we ask:

> After accounting for lag 1, does lag 2 explain anything additional?

Conceptually, we are looking at:

```text
What lag 1 explains
        +
What lag 2 additionally explains
```

The coefficient associated with lag 2 represents the partial relationship at lag 2.

That is why PACF is sometimes explained using regression.

---

# 11. An intuitive numerical example

Suppose we discover:

```text
Today's sales
```

are strongly related to:

```text
Yesterday's sales
```

Suppose:

$$
ACF(1)=0.8
$$

Now we check lag 2:

$$
ACF(2)=0.7
$$

At first glance, 0.7 looks strong.

You might say:

> "Two days ago is strongly related to today."

But wait.

Yesterday is already strongly related to today.

Maybe:

```text
2 days ago
     ↓
Yesterday
     ↓
Today
```

is causing the relationship.

So we calculate PACF.

Suppose:

$$
PACF(2)=0.05
$$

Now the interpretation changes:

> Although lag 2 has a strong overall autocorrelation, after accounting for lag 1, its **direct/partial relationship is very small**.

That's the power of PACF.

---

# 12. Another example

Suppose:

```text
ACF:

Lag 1 = 0.80
Lag 2 = 0.65
Lag 3 = 0.50
Lag 4 = 0.35
```

This looks like many lags are correlated.

But PACF might be:

```text
PACF:

Lag 1 = 0.80
Lag 2 = 0.05
Lag 3 = 0.02
Lag 4 = 0.01
```

This suggests:

```text
Lag 1 → strong direct relationship

Lag 2 → little additional direct relationship
Lag 3 → little additional direct relationship
Lag 4 → little additional direct relationship
```

The high ACF values at lags 2, 3, 4 can largely arise from the dependence propagating through earlier lags.

---

# 13. Now the opposite example

Suppose:

```text
ACF:

Lag 1 = 0.70
Lag 2 = 0.55
Lag 3 = 0.40
```

And PACF:

```text
Lag 1 = 0.70
Lag 2 = 0.50
Lag 3 = 0.03
```

Now lag 2 remains strong **even after controlling for lag 1**.

That gives evidence that lag 2 has an important direct relationship with the current value.

# 17. Why is PACF useful in AR models?

This is where PACF becomes especially useful.

Suppose you are considering an autoregressive model:

$$
Y_t = c+\phi_1Y_{t-1}+\phi_2Y_{t-2}+\cdots+\epsilon_t
$$

You have to decide:

> How many past values should I use?

Should it be:

```text
AR(1)
AR(2)
AR(3)
AR(4)
...
```

PACF can provide useful evidence about this.

For example, suppose PACF looks like:

```text
Lag 1 → significant
Lag 2 → significant
Lag 3 → significant
Lag 4 → insignificant
Lag 5 → insignificant
```

This suggests that the first three lags have meaningful direct relationships.

That can be a clue toward considering:

$$
AR(3)
$$

But **don't interpret the PACF alone as an automatic command to use AR(3)**. Model selection should also consider validation/error and the broader time-series structure.

# 18. The famous ACF/PACF intuition

For introductory ARIMA identification, you'll often hear:

### AR-type behavior

PACF tends to show a **cutoff** after a certain lag, while ACF often tails off.

### MA-type behavior

ACF tends to show a **cutoff**, while PACF often tails off.

This is a common diagnostic pattern, but real-world data can be messier, so you shouldn't treat it as an absolute rule.

You have:


ACF asks:

> "Is Monday related to Thursday?"

PACF asks:

> "After accounting for Tuesday and Wednesday, does Monday still have a direct relationship with Thursday?"

So:

### ACF

```text
"Are they connected somehow?"
```

### PACF

```text
"Are they directly connected?"
```

That's the intuition you should carry into the exam.

# 20. ACF vs PACF — final understanding

|                   | **ACF**                                        | **PACF**                                              |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Full name         | Autocorrelation Function                       | Partial Autocorrelation Function                      |
| Question          | Is current value correlated with a past value? | Is current value directly related to that past value? |
| Intermediate lags | Not removed                                    | Controlled for                                        |
| Direct effect     | Not isolated                                   | Focused on                                            |
| Lag 1             | Current ↔ previous                             | Same basic relationship                               |
| Lag 2             | Current ↔ 2-back                               | Current ↔ 2-back after accounting for lag 1           |
| Lag 3             | Current ↔ 3-back                               | Current ↔ 3-back after accounting for lags 1 & 2      |
| Main use          | Understand overall lag dependence              | Identify potentially important direct lags            |

---

> **PACF at lag \(k\) tells us how strongly the current observation is directly related to the observation \(k\) periods ago, after accounting for the effects of the lags between them.**

```text
        and Y(t-1)              │

                                ↓

```

### Subpart 1 - Autocorrelation Function (ACF) Plot

The autocorrelation function (ACF) plot shows the autocorrelation values of a time series for different lag orders. Let's import the *plot_acf* method from *statsmodels* to implement this.

# Import 'plot_acf' from 'statsmodels' to compute and visualise the autocorrelation function (ACF) for the time series
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf

# Use the 'plot_acf' method to look at the autocorrelation values for the training data for various lag orders
# Note: The 'plot_acf' method assumes stationarity of time series, so we will use the 'df_boxcox_diff' data
# Note: Since differencing results in a missing value at the beginning, we must exclude it
plot_acf(x = df_boxcox_diff[1:], color = 'blue', vlines_kwargs = {'colors': 'blue'});

### Subpart 2 - Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) Plot

The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plot shows the partial autocorrelation values of a time series for different lag orders. Partial autocorrelation is computed such that it ignores short-term correlations in the data. Let's import the *plot_pacf* method from *statsmodels* to implement this.

# Import 'plot_pacf' from 'statsmodels' to compute and visualise the partial autocorrelation function (ACF) for the time series
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_pacf

# Use the 'plot_pacf' method to look at the partial autocorrelation values for the training data for various lag orders
# Note: The 'plot_pacf' method assumes stationarity of time series, so we will use the 'df_boxcox_diff' data
# Note: Since differencing results in a missing value at the beginning, we must exclude it
plot_pacf(x = df_boxcox_diff[1:], color = 'blue', vlines_kwargs = {'colors': 'blue'});
