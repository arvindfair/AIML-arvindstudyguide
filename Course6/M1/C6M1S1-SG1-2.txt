Beyond unavoidable random error, two major components contribute to a model’s performance: 

Bias: inaccuracies arising from overly restrictive assumptions that prevent capturing true patterns in the data 

Variance: instability caused by models that adapt too closely to training data and fail to generalise 

 

A model that memorises every detail of the training set may drastically change its predictions when even a small portion of data changes, reflecting high variance. Conversely, a model that is too simplistic may consistently produce inaccurate results on unseen data, indicating high bias. 

 

Achieving the right balance between these forces is crucial. We will discuss this next. 

 

Additional Note: Understanding the Target (Bullseye) Analogy
At the end of the video, the examples use a target (bullseye) diagram to illustrate the concepts of bias and variance.

 

In this analogy:

Each point on the target represents a prediction made by the machine learning model.
The centre of the target represents the true or expected value.
 

To interpret the diagrams:

Bias indicates how close the predictions are, on average, to the centre of the target.
Predictions centred around the bullseye indicate low bias.
Predictions consistently away from the bullseye indicate high bias.
 
Variance indicates how much the predictions vary from one another.
Predictions that are tightly clustered indicate low variance.
Predictions that are widely scattered indicate high variance.
 

When interpreting each target diagram, consider the following:

How far is the overall cluster of points from the centre? This indicates the level of bias.
How closely are the points grouped together? This indicates the level of variance.
 

Using these two observations together will help you identify whether the model exhibits high or low bias and high or low variance in each example.

Bias measures how much the model’s average predictions differ from the true underlying relationship. Mathematically, for a prediction function (Equation) approximating the true function (Equation): 
 

Equation

 

A model with high bias: 

Assumes an overly simplified structure, such as a linear model for nonlinear data 

Underfits patterns and performs poorly on both training and test data 

Produces predictions that are consistently incorrect in a similar way 

 

High bias in a model can be due to excessive regularisation, limited model capacity, or restrictive assumptions. 

 

 

Variance captures how sensitive the model is to fluctuations in the training data: 
 

Equation

 

A model with high variance: 

Fits noise and random fluctuations within training samples 

Performs extremely well on the training set, but poorly on unseen data 

Changes significantly when trained on a slightly different dataset 

 

High variance can be caused by overly complex models, insufficient training samples, or weak regularisation. 

 

 

The Bias-Variance Trade-off 

 

Improving one component often worsens the other. Increasing model flexibility tends to reduce bias but raises variance. Conversely, constraining a model reduces variance, but risks increasing bias. 

 

Graphically, this relationship is typically represented as: 

Training error decreases with higher complexity 

Test error reduces initially, but then rises again as variance becomes dominant 

image 
The goal of model design and selection is to find the optimal level of complexity that minimises overall error, not just training error. 

 

As we move toward Support Vector Machines, you will see how maximising the decision margin is an explicit strategy to control variance while maintaining sufficient model expressiveness. This mathematical foundation helps explain why SVMs perform strongly even in high-dimensional classification tasks.
