Module Overview


Welcome to the module titled 'Transformers for Language Models'. 


Language modelling has evolved from the usage of fixed word representations and sequential neural architectures to models that can represent meaning using full sentence context. Earlier approaches, including classical feature-based models and recurrent neural networks, process language in a step-by-step manner and rely on limited contextual windows, making it difficult to capture long-range dependencies and complex interactions between words. 


In this module, we will explore how attention-based architectures overcome these limitations by enabling direct interactions between all parts of a sequence. It traces the progression from word-level representations and sequence-to-sequence models to fully attention-based Transformer architectures, which now form the foundation of most modern language technologies.

You learnt that word embeddings represent each word as a fixed-length vector, where semantic relationships are reflected through geometric closeness in the embedding space. Words that appear in similar contexts tend to receive similar vector representations. 

 

You explored several widely used methods for learning such embeddings: 

Word2Vec, which learns embeddings using prediction-based training objectives: 

Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) predicts a target word from the surrounding context words. This encourages the model to learn how groups of related words jointly indicate meaning. 
Skip-gram predicts surrounding context words from a given target word. This helps the model learn strong representations even for less frequent words.
 
GloVe, which uses global word co-occurrence statistics to learn embeddings that reflect overall distributional patterns across the corpus.
 

FastText, which represents words using character n-grams, allowing it to model internal word structure and handle rare or unseen words more effectively. 

 

Although all these approaches capture useful word-level semantics, they assign only one fixed vector to each word, regardless of how that word is used in different sentences. This leads to important limitations that affect how meaning is modelled in real language use, which we will cover next.

# 1. The big picture: How language models evolved

Imagine you ask a computer:

"What is the meaning of the word bank?"

A computer doesn't naturally understand the word. It needs to convert language into numbers.

Over time, NLP evolved through several stages.

## Evolution of language models

1. One-hot encoding
   Words become large vectors containing mostly zeros.
   Simple numerical representation
2. Word embeddings
   Words become meaningful, dense numerical vectors.
   Semantic relationships
3. RNN / Encoder–Decoder
   Process words sequentially, one after another.
   Sequence learning
4. Attention mechanism
   The model learns which words are important to other words.
   Better context handling
5. Transformers
   Use attention to process relationships across the entire sequence.
   Foundation of modern LLMs

Think of it this way:

- One-hot encoding: Knows the word's identity.
- Word embeddings: Understands relationships between words.
- RNN: Reads words one by one.
- Attention: Looks at relevant words directly.
- Transformer: Uses attention as its main mechanism to understand context efficiently.

Pizza and Burger often appear in similar contexts.

# 4. CBOW — Continuous Bag of Words

Imagine I give you this sentence:

"The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog."

But I remove one word:

"The quick \_\_\_ fox jumps."

Can you guess the missing word?

Probably "brown".

That's exactly what CBOW does.

CBOW = Surrounding words → Predict the middle word.

### How does CBOW work?

Step 1: Take surrounding words.

Step 2: Convert each word into an embedding vector.

Step 3: Average or sum all the vectors.

Step 4: Pass the combined representation through an output layer.

Step 5: Predict the missing word.

### What does Bag of Words mean?

CBOW does not consider the order of context words when combining their vectors.

For example:

"The quick brown fox"

and

"The fox quick brown"

would have the same combined representation if they contain the same context words.

That's why it is called a Bag of Words.

# 5. Skip-gram — The reverse of CBOW

Now let's reverse the process.

Instead of giving the computer surrounding words and asking it to predict the middle word, we give it one word and ask it to predict surrounding words.

Skip-gram = Middle word → Predict surrounding words.

For example:

Input: Brown

Predict:

- The
- Quick
- Fox
- Jumps

### How does Skip-gram work?

Step 1: Take one target word.

Step 2: Convert it into an embedding.

Step 3: Predict words that appear nearby.

Step 4: Calculate prediction errors.

Step 5: Update the embedding to improve future predictions.

Unlike CBOW, Skip-gram creates multiple input-output training pairs from one target word.

For example:

| Input | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Brown | The |
| Brown | Quick |
| Brown | Fox |
| Brown | Jumps |

### Why is Skip-gram useful?

Because it learns individual word-context relationships, it often performs well for rare words.

# 7. GloVe — Global Vectors

Now let's learn another embedding technique.

Word2Vec learns by predicting words using nearby context.

But GloVe takes a different approach.

It looks at how frequently words appear together across the entire dataset.

Imagine we have a collection of 1 million sentences.

We create a co-occurrence matrix.

## What is a co-occurrence matrix?

It counts how often two words appear near each other.

For example:

| Word | Ice | Steam | Water |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | 50 | 2 | 20 |
| Gas | 1 | 40 | 15 |
| Liquid | 20 | 15 | 50 |

GloVe uses these global statistics to learn word vectors.

The central idea:

Words that have similar co-occurrence patterns tend to have similar representations.

### Word2Vec vs GloVe

- Word2Vec: Learns through local context prediction.
- GloVe: Learns through global co-occurrence statistics.

An easy analogy:

Imagine Word2Vec is a student reading sentences one by one.

GloVe is a student looking at a statistical summary of the entire library.

# 8. FastText — Understanding parts of words

FastText solves this by breaking words into smaller character groups called character n-grams.

# 10. The biggest limitation of traditional word embeddings

Now comes the most important concept because it explains WHY Transformers became necessary.

Consider the word:

BANK

Sentence 1: I deposited money in the bank.

Sentence 2: I sat near the river bank.

Does BANK mean the same thing in both sentences?

No\!

- Sentence 1: Financial institution.
- Sentence 2: Side of a river.

But traditional Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText assign one fixed embedding to the word "bank".

## The contextual meaning problem

Sentence 1 I deposited money in the BANK.

Financial institutionTraditional embedding: BANK → Vector A

Sentence 2 I sat near the river BANK.

River sideTraditional embedding: BANK → Vector A

Problem

## Same vector, different meanings

This is called the limitation of static word embeddings.

They cannot dynamically change a word's representation based on the sentence.

## How do Transformers solve this?

Transformers use contextual representations.

Instead of giving BANK one permanent meaning, they examine the surrounding words.

You learnt that traditional word embeddings assign the same vector to a word across all contexts, which leads to several limitations; these are described below: 

Context insensitivity: A word receives the same representation even when its meaning changes, such as 'bank' referring to a financial institution or a river edge.

 

Inability to capture grammatical and entity roles: The same word vector is used whether a word appears as a common noun, a named entity or in different syntactic positions. 

 

Limited the modelling of long-range dependencies: Training relies mainly on local context windows, which makes it difficult to capture relationships across longer spans of text. 
 

Poor handling of sentence-level meaning: Subtle meanings involving negation, emphasis or sarcasm are not captured at the word level alone. 

 

These issues arise because meaning is influenced by surrounding words and sentence structure, not just by individual word usage. Addressing these challenges requires models that generate context-dependent representations, where the embedding of a word changes based on its usage within a sentence.  

 

This motivates models that produce representations that change based on surrounding words and sentence-level interactions, which will be covered in upcoming sessions. 

# Limitations of Traditional Word Embeddings – Explained Visually

Let's understand this as if we are building an NLP application.

First, remember the three traditional word embedding techniques:

1. Word2Vec
2. GloVe (Global Vectors)
3. FastText

All three revolutionized NLP by converting words into numerical vectors.

But they have one fundamental limitation:

They assign one fixed vector to a word, irrespective of the sentence in which it appears.

This is the starting point for understanding all four limitations.

## 1. First understand how Word Embeddings work

Computers cannot directly understand words like `Apple`, `Bank`, or `King`.

So we convert words into numerical vectors.

For example, imagine Word2Vec generates these vectors:

| Word  | Vector (simplified) |
| ----- | ------------------- |
| King  | [0.8, 0.9, 0.2]     |
| Queen | [0.7, 0.9, 0.3]     |
| Apple | [0.1, 0.3, 0.9]     |
| Bank  | [0.4, 0.6, 0.2]     |

These numbers represent learned semantic relationships.

Words with similar meanings tend to have vectors closer together in the embedding space.

Why does this happen?

During training, the model learns from surrounding words.

- In financial sentences, BANK appears near money, deposit, cheque, loan.
- In geographical sentences, BANK appears near river, water, shore, edge.

Both sets of contexts influence the same bank vector.

The vector may encode a mixture of the two usages. It is not necessarily a literal mathematical average, but it cannot create two separate representations for the same word.

The problem: The model cannot dynamically decide which meaning of BANK is being used.

Modern models such as BERT generate different representations depending on surrounding words.

[0.82, 0.15, 0.64]

BANK → River meaning

[0.12, 0.87, 0.35]

Illustrative vectors: BERT calculates context-dependent representations rather than using a single fixed lookup vector.

Remember for exams: Word2Vec, GloVe and FastText are context-independent embeddings. BERT is a contextual embedding model.

## 4. Limitation 3: Limited context and long-range dependencies

Can capture nearby words, struggles with distant relationships

This is one of the most important limitations to understand.

But consider this paragraph:

What does it refer to?

The intended reference is the experiment or its results, depending on the precise sentence structure.

A model needs to understand relationships across multiple sentences to resolve such references.

### What is a long-range dependency?

It means one word's interpretation depends on another word that appears much earlier or later in the sentence or document.

Examples:

- Pronoun resolution: John bought a car because he needed it.
- Subject-verb relationships: The list of items on the table is long.
- Understanding references across paragraphs.
- Connecting a question to information in an earlier paragraph.

Why does this matter?

Traditional word embeddings are not designed to understand the complete sentence or document.

They are useful for learning local word associations, but they do not maintain a representation of the entire paragraph's evolving meaning.

This affects:

- Document summarization
- Reading comprehension
- Question answering
- Coreference resolution

### How Transformers improve this

Transformers use a mechanism called Self-Attention.

Instead of relying only on nearby words, self-attention allows each token to calculate how relevant other tokens are to it within the available input.

For example, the word `it` can pay attention to `experiment`, `scientist`, or `results`, depending on the sentence.

## 5. Limitation 4: Poor handling of implicit meaning, negation and sarcasm

Word meaning ≠ Sentence meaning

This is particularly interesting because humans understand things that are not explicitly written.

Let's compare two sentences.

Sentence A: Literal positive meaning

"The movie is good."

Positive sentiment

Sentence B: Sarcastic meaning

"The movie is so good it cured my insomnia."

Negative sentiment (sarcastic)

What the person really means: The movie was so boring that it made them sleepy.

Look at the important words:

| Word     | Typical association |
| -------- | ------------------- |
| Good     | Positive            |
| Cured    | Positive            |
| Insomnia | Negative condition  |

A simplistic sentiment system that averages individual word scores may classify the second sentence as positive because of words like `good` and `cured`.

But a human understands the irony.

### Another important example: Negation

Compare:

1. The food is good.
2. The food is not good.

Only one word changed: `not`.

But the sentiment changed from positive to negative.

A simple word embedding does not itself perform sentence-level sentiment reasoning. A downstream model might learn negation patterns, but a static embedding alone cannot dynamically change the representation of `good` based on `not`.

Important: Traditional embeddings do not always fail at sarcasm or negation. A classifier trained with sufficient examples and suitable features may learn some patterns. The fundamental limitation is that the embeddings themselves do not encode the complete contextual interpretation.
