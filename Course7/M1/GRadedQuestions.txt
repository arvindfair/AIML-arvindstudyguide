**Question:**
A 3x3 Gaussian blur kernel includes a normalisation factor (e.g., 1/16). Why is this factor necessary?

**Answer:**
To preserve the overall brightness of the image after filtering.

**Explanation:**
When applying a filter like a Gaussian blur, the new value of a pixel is calculated by taking a weighted sum of its neighboring pixels. The weights are defined by the kernel matrix. If the sum of all the values in this kernel is greater than 1, the resulting pixel values will systematically increase, causing the output image to become brighter than the original. By multiplying the kernel by a normalisation factor (such as 1/16 for a standard integer-based 3x3 Gaussian kernel), it ensures that the sum of all weights equals exactly 1. This guarantees that regions of uniform intensity remain unchanged, effectively preserving the original overall brightness of the image.

The early layers capture general visual features that remain useful across different image domains.

**Explanation:**
This scenario describes a common transfer learning technique called feature extraction. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) learn hierarchically. **Question:**
During transfer learning, a practitioner decides to unfreeze all layers and retrain the entire network using a very low learning rate. In which situation is this strategy most appropriate?

**Answer:**
When the target task differs significantly from the original pre-training task.

**Explanation:**
This technique is known as full fine-tuning. When a target dataset or task is substantially different from the original dataset the model was trained on (for example, transferring from natural images to satellite imagery), the specific high-level features—and sometimes even the mid-level features—learned during pre-training may not be suitable. By unfreezing all layers, the model is allowed to adapt its feature extractors to the new, distinct domain. A very low learning rate is critical during this process; it ensures that the network makes small, gentle updates to the weights, preserving the foundational knowledge it already has without completely destroying or overwriting the pre-trained weights (a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting). If the dataset were very small and noisy, unfreezing all layers would instead lead to severe overfitting.

**Question:**
Suppose a model produces embeddings where small visual perturbations in the input cause large, unpredictable changes in the embedding values. Which downstream issue is most likely to occur?

**Answer:**
Semantically similar inputs may appear far apart in the embedding space.

**Explanation:**
A well-trained embedding space maps inputs to continuous vectors such that semantically similar items (e.g., two pictures of a cat from slightly different angles) are located close to one another. If a model is overly sensitive to tiny visual changes (perturbations or noise), a picture of a cat and that same picture with a few altered pixels will produce drastically different embedding vectors. Consequently, these highly similar inputs will end up far apart in the embedding space, ruining the model's ability to reliably classify, cluster, or retrieve similar images downstream.


**Question:**
A decoder attempts to reconstruct fine object boundaries but produces blurred outputs, even though the encoder is deep and expressive. Which explanation best accounts for this outcome?

**Answer:**
Spatial detail lost during encoding cannot be fully recovered by decoding alone.

**Explanation:**
In a standard encoder-decoder architecture, the encoder progressively downsamples the input image to extract deep, abstract semantic features. While this makes the network "expressive" in understanding *what* is in the image, it inherently destroys fine spatial resolution—meaning it forgets exactly *where* the sharp edges and boundaries are. When the decoder attempts to reconstruct the image from this compressed, low-resolution latent representation, it has to "guess" where those fine details belong, which typically results in blurred boundaries. This exact limitation is why architectures like U-Net use "skip connections" to pass high-resolution spatial information directly from the early layers of the encoder to the decoder.
