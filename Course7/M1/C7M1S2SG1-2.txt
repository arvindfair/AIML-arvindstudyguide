Object Detection

Session Overview

In this session, we are going to study object detection as a core computer vision capability that extends beyond image classification. Many real-world applications require not only identifying what is present in an image, but also locating multiple objects accurately.  

 

Object detection relies directly on the image representations and features you have already learned, and it forms a foundation for more advanced vision and generative systems. We will focus on the key ideas that make object detection possible, such as bounding boxes, localisation, and evaluation metrics.  

 

Along the way, we will look at different modelling approaches used for detection to understand the trade-offs between accuracy and speed, and why modern systems favour end-to-end, real-time designs. By the end of the session, you should have a clear picture of the ideas behind working of object detection techniques and why these basic concepts are essential for building scalable vision systems.

Object localization involves identifying the correct bounding box that is as close to the contours of the target as possible. The detection in itself is confirmed through the bounding box, which is a very important feature in object detection. The bounding box in itself is represented by 4 attributes, that is, the X&Y coordinates of its centroid, the bounding box height, and the bounding box width. The bounding boxes do not only serve the purpose of delivering an output but also for the purpose of evaluation. A typical metric utilizing bounding boxes for evaluating the performance of our network is known as the intersection of the union, or IOU. Given a bounding box from our predictor and a bounding box from the ground truth, it is possible to combine them in a way that would allow us to see how close the 2 are together and assets understand in a way how good our network is performing. The formula for doing so involves two different metrics. At the top of this division we have the area of the intersection of the two bounding boxes, and at the bottom the area of their union. The area of the intersection is essentially the common area of the two bounding boxes, while the area of the union is very much the area of the two combined. An example of two of these bounding boxes placed on an image and a test limits is shown on the right. So you see the ground truth being represented by the red bounding box and the different bounding box shown in green. As you see the two of them have quite a difference and as such in this specific example one would expect that the IOU would be approximately 70%. In practice, this is very much the minimum IOU that one can tolerate.

Now, since the output of an object detection model is primarily a bounding box, you need some method to be able to evaluate how well the model has localised an object and predicted its bounding box.

 

The standard industry metric that you need to consider for this purpose is intersection over union (IoU). This metric evaluates the accuracy of your model’s prediction of the bounding box by comparing it with the ‘ground truth’ bounding box present in the training set. Take a look at this image, which shows the predicted and ground truth bounding boxes.



Recall that this is similar to the actual and predicted outputs, which you have been comparing in the case of ML and DL algorithms so far. However, instead of having a single value of the outputs, in the case of object detection algorithms, you will have a vector. 

 

The IoU is measured by taking the intersection of the two boxes and dividing it by their union. It can be expressed as follows:

IoU = Area of intersection / Area of union

Introduction to Loss Functions in Object Localization
We briefly mentioned the loss functions before. The regression in object localization typically uses the L1 or L2 loss functions for the purpose of error minimization. The objective is very much to move the predicted bounding box as close as possible to the real 1.
00:00:21 - 00:01:30
Comparison of L1 and L2 Loss Functions
So essentially we are having the displacement of the centroid of the predicted bounding box towards the centre of the one provided in the ground truth. Briefly, the L1 loss function is used to minimize the error which is expressed as the sum of the absolute differences between predicted values and the ground truth values. In the L2 function we have a slightly different formula. We are having the squared differences between the two values. The objective in both cases is to minimize the error between the predicted and actual central location of each of the objects detected. And in reality, L2 is highly preferred compared to L1. However, this assumes that we do not have outliers in our data set. If that is the case, the L2 tends to underperform compared to L1 due to the square differences.
